## 1. Who does which back-office job, how often and where; time spent on admin; what they most want automated

### Takeaway
No independent time-use or task-frequency study of restaurant or shop managers was found; the numbers in circulation are vendor surveys that put manager admin somewhere between "a few hours" and 8 to 10 hours a week, with rota and scheduling the most cited single burden. The one behavioural source (a US vendor's assistant logs) shows managers asking short sales and staffing questions from a phone during service, while heavy setup work such as menu builds stays on an office computer.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The telemetry supports a task-to-device split rather than one interface for everything: in service, managers want a fast answer or a small staffing change on the phone in their hand; catalogue and setup work (menus, prices) happens seated, on a large screen, at a quieter time. An assistant that tries to move menu building onto a phone chat is working against observed behaviour.
- Only 15% of prompts arrive at nightly close. Cash-up and close-down are routine, form-shaped jobs done when people are tired and want to leave; the data gives no sign that managers want to talk their way through them. A guided, pre-filled form is the better bet there, with the assistant in support.
- Rota and scheduling is the best-evidenced weekly pain (most adopted AI use among UK managers, most cited time sink), and ordering and stock the best-evidenced payback. Those two are the natural first places for assistant-led work.
- The daily, weekly and monthly cadence in the brief (cash-up, checklists, sold-out items daily; rota, ordering, wages, stock count weekly; reports, menu and price changes monthly) is standard industry practice and matches these sources indirectly, but no source found here measures it.

### Gaps
- No independent time-and-motion or diary study of restaurant, QSR or shop managers was found. All admin-hours figures are vendor surveys with unknown or self-selected samples.
- No sourced role-by-task matrix (owner, area manager, GM, shift lead, chef, bookkeeper). The query aimed at this, and at "what managers most want automated", was not run (search budget).
- The Fourth and QSR Magazine "State of Restaurant Operations 2026" benchmark (n=112, US) has a section on GM and AGM weekly admin burden that the extract did not show: [PDF](https://www.fourth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/State_of_Restaurant_Operations_2026.pdf). Worth reading in full.

## 2. Device and environment reality: phone, tablet and desktop share; shared devices and logins; gloves, noise, connectivity; voice interfaces and wearables

### Takeaway
Phones already carry a little over half of assistant use in the one dataset available, shared devices and shared logins are the norm on the shop and restaurant floor, and kitchens are loud enough (commonly 80 to 95 dB(A) at cooking stations) to degrade speech recognition. Evidence that staff-facing voice works in kitchens is vendor claim only; the concrete 2026 deployments of staff voice assistants are in retail, on headsets or handhelds with a typed fallback, and none has published results.

### Cited Findings
Device share
- Mobile devices accounted for 54% of all prompts to a restaurant POS vendor's AI assistant; fast-casual operators sent 58% from phones. [US | vendor telemetry | 2026] — [Toast Q1 2026 trends](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/data/q1-2026-restaurant-ai-pos-trends)
- 34% of US retail employees (7.7 million store staff) have no mobile device for their exclusive use. [US | analyst study commissioned by a device vendor | date not shown, likely older] — [Chain Store Age](https://chainstoreage.com/study-retailers-fall-short-giving-employees-dedicated-mobile-devices-0)
- Frontline mobile workers rely on an average of five or more separate apps, up more than 80% in 24 months. [country not stated | analyst and vendor research | date not shown] — [BlueFletch](https://bluefletch.com/press/new-research-reveals-critical-gaps-in-mobile-technology-for-frontline-workers/)
- Only 35% of workers globally feel empowered by their company's app or scheduling tool. [global | vendor report | sample not shown] — [Workvivo](https://www.workvivo.com/blog/frontline-gap-tech-gap/)
- 45% of frontline workers believe technology has already made their daily job easier. [country not confirmed, probably US | vendor survey | n=1,014 frontline workers | June 2026] — [HCA Magazine](https://www.hcamag.com/au/news/general/frontline-workers-feel-replaceable-despite-strong-job-pride/587928)

[multi-country | survey sponsored by an identity vendor and a standards body | n=700 | October 2026] — [Help Net Security](https://helpnetsecurity.com/2026/10/20/frontline-worker-authentication-passwordless); [FIDO Alliance report PDF](https://fidoalliance.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/10/Frontline-Friction-The-State-of-Physical-and-Digital-Identity-in-the-Enterprise.pdf)

[vendor descriptions, not measurements] — [Ping Identity](https://www.pingidentity.com/en/resources/blog/post/verifying-trust-frontline-workers.html); [Microsoft Intune blog](https://techcommunity.microsoft.com/blog/IntuneCustomerSuccess/from-the-frontlines-accelerating-retail-worker-shared-device-experience-part-one/4397212)

Noise
- Chief cooks in Hong Kong Chinese restaurants had a daily personal noise exposure of 95.4 dB(A), with readings up to 108 dB(A); cooking areas averaged 86.9 dB(A). [Hong Kong | peer-reviewed study (2013, older) reported through a secondary blog: check the paper] — [VoxBooster summary](https://voxbooster.com/blog/commercial-kitchen-dishwasher-noise-exposure-statistics-2026)

Surfaced in search extracts, page attribution not confirmed (candidate link given; verify before use)
- A UK convenience retailer signed a five-year deal to put headsets in more than 2,300 stores after a 114-store pilot, rolling out from February 2026; and a "State of the Connected Store 2026" report found 79% of retailers admit staff must leave the customer's side to use store systems. [UK | vendor] — candidate: [VoCoVo article](https://www.vocovo.com/en-GB/resources/articles/why-2026-is-the-year-we-started-conversing-with-ai-in-retail)

### Inferences
- Design for the shared device first. The host-stand tablet and the kitchen screen are used by whoever is nearest, often left signed in. Anything an assistant changes from such a device needs a cheap way to say who is acting (a PIN or tap, not a full login), or the audit trail is worthless. The measured cost of login friction (2.5 hours per affected worker per month) says heavy authentication will simply be worked around.
- At 80 to 95 dB(A), open-microphone voice at a cooking station is the hardest case there is. The retail deployments that exist put the microphone at the mouth (headset) or in the hand (handheld), and keep typing as an equal route. That is the pattern to copy: hold to talk, close microphone, typed or tapped fallback always present, spoken numbers read back before they are saved.
- Voice is strongest where hands are busy and the vocabulary is small and checkable (a temperature, a quantity, "86 the sea bass"), weakest for free-form requests full of names (dishes, suppliers, staff), which is exactly where recognition research shows errors concentrate.
- Over half of assistant use is already on phones, and a third of US store staff have no work device of their own: personal phones are in practice part of the estate, which brings their own limits (staff will not all install an app; some sites restrict phones on the floor).

### Gaps
- Tablet versus phone versus desktop share for back-office tasks across the industry: only one vendor's telemetry found, and it covers assistant prompts, not all back-office work.
- Gloves, wet or greasy hands and touchscreen failure; kitchen and shop-floor connectivity (Wi-Fi dead spots, offline working): no sources found in the searches run.
- Voice in warehouses (voice-directed picking accuracy and productivity, push-to-talk, headset practice) and wearables (smartwatches, badges) in restaurants and shops: the query was not run (search budget). This is the most mature body of evidence on industrial voice and should be added.
- No independent, controlled measurement of speech recognition accuracy for staff in a working commercial kitchen was found. A test in the client's own venues at peak would be the only reliable figure.
- A vendor survey figure that fewer than six in ten frontline workers use a mobile device at work and 73% still use paper forms, and a 2025 manufacturing figure that 64% of frontline workers face smartphone restrictions on the floor, both surfaced in extracts but could not be tied to a confirmed page.

## 3. Workforce characteristics that shape UX: turnover, training time, multilingual teams, age, digital literacy, accessibility; implications for natural-language input

### Takeaway
The UK hospitality frontline turns over at roughly two-thirds a year, a large share of starters are gone inside 90 days, and migrants are heavily over-represented, especially back of house; so at any moment a big part of the user base is new, lightly trained and often working in a second language. Speech recognition carries a measurable penalty for non-native speakers, which newer models and an LLM correction pass narrow but do not remove.

### Cited Findings
- Team turnover in UK hospitality fell from 75% to 67% in the year to autumn 2025, helped by more internal promotion. [UK | report by a recruitment group and a workforce software vendor | sample not shown | September 2025] — [Restaurant Online](https://www.restaurantonline.co.uk/Article/2025/09/30/internal-promotions-driving-lower-staff-turnover-in-hospitality-report-says); [Craft Guild of Chefs](https://craftguildofchefs.org/news/report-finds-hospitality-staff-turnover-drops-almost-10)
- Only about one in four hospitality staff pass the 90-day mark, and training costs have risen about 70% over ten years. [UK | payments vendor's index citing a workforce vendor's 2026 report | May 2026] — [Morning Advertiser](https://www.morningadvertiser.co.uk/Article/2026/05/29/only-1-in-4-staff-remain-in-work-after-90-days-training-costs-up-70/); [Startups.co.uk](https://startups.co.uk/news/hospitality-training-costs/); contradicted in scale by [Access Group](https://www.theaccessgroup.com/en-gb/hospitality/resources/hospitality-staff-and-hr-resources/reduce-staff-turnover/) (around 30% leave in the first 90 days) and [All Gravy](https://allgravy.com/hospitality-retention-benchmark-2026) (20 to 30%). The two sets of figures cannot both be right as stated; all are vendor sources. The safe statement is "a substantial share of new starters, between a quarter and three quarters depending on source, leave within 90 days".
- Manager churn is high too: only 43% of US operators report GM and assistant GM turnover below 50%. [US | vendor and trade magazine benchmark | n=112 restaurant leaders | 2026] — [Fourth PDF](https://www.fourth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/State_of_Restaurant_Operations_2026.pdf)
- In December 2025, 19% of UK employees (5.9 million) were adult migrants, and migrants are over-represented in hospitality; foreign-born workers are more likely to work nights and in non-permanent jobs. [UK | university research unit using official data | 2026 briefing] — [Migration Observatory](https://migrationobservatory.ox.ac.uk/resources/briefings/migrants-in-the-uk-labour-market-an-overview)
- In London, hospitality has the highest share of payrolled jobs held by nationals from outside the UK and EU, 39%. [London only | city government analysis of tax data | 2026] — [London Datastore](https://data.london.gov.uk/blog/payrolled-employments-by-nationality-2026)
- 57% of UK restaurant workers were from overseas, most of all back of house. [UK | workforce vendor's analysis of 25,000 employees | about 2017, old and pre-dating post-Brexit rules] — [The Caterer](https://thecaterer.com/articles/493244/more-than-half-of-restaurant-workers-in-the-uk-are-from-overseas)
- Hospitality staff are "often young and from minority ethnic backgrounds or born overseas". [UK | charity report | 2025] — [Unseen](https://www.unseenuk.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/09/Unseen_Hospitality-report_.pdf)
- 25.2% of UK hospitality workers are aged 50 or over. [UK | newspaper figure via secondary coverage | date not shown] — [IBTimes UK](https://www.ibtimes.co.uk/around-64-uk-hospitality-managers-will-tackle-staff-shortages-hiring-older-workers-1717399)
- Few frontline staff have any AI practice: nearly three-quarters of UK retail staff have no experience of using AI in their role. [UK | business body poll | sample not shown | 2026 cycle] — [Retail Times](https://retailtimes.co.uk/retail-staff-being-left-behind-on-ai/). Half of UK retail workers have used generative AI at work and fewer than a quarter say their employer has trained them. [UK | professional-services survey, fieldwork by a polling company | 25,000 UK workers, all sectors | 7 May to 10 June 2026] — [Resultsense](https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-11-16-deloitte-retail-workers-genai-time-savings/). These two figures conflict (about a quarter versus half have used AI); the samples, dates and question wording differ.
- Speech recognition and non-native speakers: with one widely used model, the error rate for non-native speakers ran about 11 points above native speakers; a newer variant narrowed the gap to about 3.4 points, and an LLM correction pass to about 1.7 points. [peer-reviewed, medical dictation setting | 2025] — [GCN](https://gcn.com/?p=23126)
- Newer recognisers have the lowest error rates overall but second-language speakers still score worse, and speakers whose first language is tonal (Mandarin, Vietnamese) are at a further disadvantage. [academic workshop paper | 2025] — [ISCA SLaTE 2025](https://www.isca-archive.org/slate_2025/roll25_slate.pdf)
- Open-source recognisers degrade on noisy non-native dialogue; multimodal LLMs cope better with accent but struggle with named entities. [academic preprint | 2025] — [arXiv 2511.14255](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.14255v1)

### Inferences
- With two-thirds annual turnover and heavy early leaving, "training" cannot be a course: the interface is the training. Anything that needs remembering (where a setting lives, the right word for a thing) will be forgotten by the organisation every few months. This is the strongest human-factors argument for an assistant that answers "how do I" and takes people to the right place, and for task lists that tell a new starter what to do next.
- The same facts argue against free-text as the only way in for frontline staff. Second-language users can usually recognise the right option faster than they can compose a correct sentence; spelling of dish, supplier and colleague names is a known failure point for both typing and speech. Natural-language input needs forgiving matching, visible suggestions to tap, and confirmation that shows what was understood, in plain words and with the matched record named.
- Recognition errors cluster on names and in noise, which is where restaurant commands live ("move Agnieszka to Friday close", "sold out of nduja"). Biasing recognition towards the tenant's own vocabulary (menu items, staff, suppliers) and reading the match back before acting is not polish; it is what makes voice usable for this workforce.
- Managers and owners are a different population from crew: longer tenure, first-language or fluent, more desk time. A single interaction style for both groups will fit neither.

### Gaps
- English proficiency of UK hospitality and retail workers: no data found.
- Dyslexia, colour-vision deficiency and other accessibility needs in this workforce, and adult literacy and essential digital skills in the UK: the query was not run (search budget). These need sourcing (British Dyslexia Association, NHS, the UK essential digital skills surveys) before the report makes claims.
- Typing versus speaking speed and error rates on phones, especially for second-language users: not searched.
- Training time for new hospitality starters, and the share of crew under 25: a figure that 66% of waiting staff and 60% of bar staff are under 25 surfaced in an extract but could not be tied to a confirmed, dated page.
- Official statistics (ONS workforce by age, country of birth and industry) were not retrieved directly; the migration figures above are from analyses of official data, not the releases themselves.

## 4. AI adoption by operators in 2025 and 2026: who uses it, for what, satisfaction, barriers, differences by operator type

### Takeaway
Measured adoption depends almost entirely on who asks and how "using AI" is defined: the US trade body finds about 39% of restaurant operators using AI at all and 3% running it companywide, while POS vendors' own surveys report 57% (UK managers) to nearly 90% (US operators). The consistent picture underneath is broad, shallow use (marketing, content, some scheduling and ordering), strong stated enthusiasm, cautious behaviour, and barriers of cost, complexity, integration, data security and lack of training. No UKHospitality, British Retail Consortium, NRF or Retail Economics survey on AI was found in the searches run.

## 6. Management by exception and proactive briefings: do alerts, daily briefs and guided tasks beat dashboards and menus; alert fatigue

### Takeaway
No controlled evidence was found, in hospitality, retail or elsewhere, that alerts, briefs or guided tasks outperform dashboards for managers; the case rests on reasoning plus two indirect signals (operators want visibility they do not have, and managers ask assistants short questions mid-service). The alert-fatigue evidence, all from IT, security and clinical settings, is clear that volume and false alarms cause people to ignore alerts, including deliberately.

### Cited Findings
- 15% of UK IT respondents said they had deliberately ignored or suppressed alerts (13% globally); the same study links missed alerts to outages at three-quarters of UK IT teams. [UK and global | vendor-commissioned study by an economics consultancy | 2025; IT operations, not hospitality] — [Computer Weekly](https://www.computerweekly.com/news/366637587/Three-quarters-of-UK-IT-teams-beset-by-outages-due-to-missing-alerts)
- Clinical research cited by a software vendor: 72 to 99% of clinical alarms are false. [healthcare | secondary citation | older] — [Atlassian](https://www.atlassian.com/incident-management/on-call/alert-fatigue)
- A security-operations figure of about 3,832 alerts a day with 62% ignored. [secondary, unverified] — [Workato](https://www.workato.com/the-connector/alert-fatigue/)
- Practitioner view: fatigue depends on whether alerts are actionable and relevant to the person, and on how many they must review; recommended remedies are tuned thresholds, grouping duplicates, and severity tiers with low-severity items sent as passive text. [vendor guidance, not studies] — [Network World](https://www.networkworld.com/article/2919515/the-science-behind-alert-fatigue-how-to-turn-down-the-noise-so-you-can-hear-the-signal.html); [IBM](https://www.ibm.com/topics/alert-fatigue)
- Demand signal: about 80% of multi-unit restaurant operators want real-time visibility of task completion and only 36% have it; only 38% have complete real-time visibility of overtime and compliance. [US | vendor survey | 300+ | 2025] — [Crunchtime](https://www.crunchtime.com/blog/webinar-recap-2025-restaurant-growth-insights-report)
- Behaviour signal: 53% of prompts to a restaurant assistant arrive during peak service, mostly from phones, and are mostly about sales. [US | vendor telemetry | 2026] — [Toast Q1 2026 trends](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/data/q1-2026-restaurant-ai-pos-trends)
- Vendor direction: an AI engine to prioritise frontline tasks is due in the second half of 2026. [vendor announcement] — [IT Brief](https://itbrief.com.au/story/workjam-adds-autonomous-ai-layer-for-frontline-staff)

### Inferences
- The report should present "the day comes to the manager" (a brief, a short list of what needs doing, exceptions flagged) as a well-reasoned design bet, not as a proven result. It fits how these people work (interrupted, on their feet, mid-service) and what they ask for, but nobody has published a test of it against dashboards in this sector.
- The alert-fatigue lesson transfers directly and cuts against over-enthusiasm: a feed that cries wolf will be ignored within weeks, and with two-thirds staff turnover there is no long memory to rebuild trust. The design consequences are few items, each one actionable by the person seeing it, grouped rather than repeated, with severity that means something, and a quiet channel (the brief) for everything that is merely interesting. Measure the share of items acted on and prune what nobody acts on.
- Dashboards and briefs serve different people. The owner, area manager and bookkeeper at a desk still need tables to compare and drill into; the GM and shift lead need the exception. The evidence does not support replacing one with the other.

### Gaps
- Peer-reviewed alert override rates in clinical decision support (the best-measured alert-fatigue literature), and studies of notification batching and digests: the query was not run (search budget).
- Any retail or hospitality evaluation of exception reporting, daily briefs or guided tasks against dashboards: none found.

## 7. Trust, error cost and automation bias: what managers will let software do unattended, where they insist on review, over-reliance and deskilling

### Takeaway
Operators say they want AI with a person in the loop, a clear explanation, and a human to reach when it goes wrong; staff push back on AI-made rotas. The best measured evidence comes from retail replenishment, where store managers override automated orders systematically, sometimes for good reasons the system cannot see and sometimes to the business's cost, and where bounded overrides do better than either free overrides or none. Research on automation bias and deskilling exists mainly for clinical and knowledge work, not frontline hospitality.

### Cited Findings
What operators and staff say
- US operators use AI to speed up tedious work but "for the most part still want human oversight". [US | vendor survey | n=676 | April 2026] — [Toast restaurant AI report](https://pos.toasttab.com/blog/data/restaurant-ai-report)
- What would raise UK managers' trust in an AI recommendation: a clear explanation of it (26%) and independent evidence from similar restaurants (24%). [UK | vendor survey | n=400 | 2026 cycle] — [Toast UK on AI scheduling](https://pos.toasttab.com/uk/blog/on-the-line/why-ai-scheduling-succeeds-or-fails-on-how-you-explain-it)
- 65% of UK managers said staff raised concerns about AI-generated schedules frequently or occasionally. [UK | same survey] — [Toast UK on AI scheduling](https://pos.toasttab.com/uk/blog/on-the-line/why-ai-scheduling-succeeds-or-fails-on-how-you-explain-it)
- The top answer for what would most increase trust in restaurant AI was "knowing I can easily reach a human if something goes wrong": 51% of Irish managers, 48% of Canadian managers. [Ireland and Canada | vendor surveys | n=400 in Canada] — [Toast Ireland](https://pos.toasttab.com/ie/blog/on-the-line/ai-use-in-irish-restaurants-data-and-insights); [Toast Canada](https://pos.toasttab.com/ca/blog/on-the-line/ai-use-in-canadian-restaurants-data-and-insights)
- 42% of US operators describe their approach to AI as cautious; complexity is the top concern (46.1%). [US | trade body | November 2026] — [Nation's Restaurant News](https://www.nrn.com/restaurant-technology/restaurants-are-still-taking-a-cautious-approach-to-ai)
- 51% of UK and Ireland operators worry about data privacy when using AI tools. [UK and Ireland | vendor study | March 2026] — [SecurityBrief UK](https://securitybrief.co.uk/story/uk-hospitality-operators-worry-over-ai-data-security)

What managers actually do with automated decisions (measured)
- Supermarket store managers systematically changed automated order advice (19,417 item and store combinations), moving orders from peak to quieter days; their changes reflected two things the system ignored, in-store handling cost and the sales benefit of fuller shelves. [Europe | peer-reviewed, Management Science | 2010, older but foundational] — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.1090.1141)
- A field study of algorithmic order recommendations found managers deviate on both when and how much to order, and identified a "compliance trap": when the algorithm is most accurate and following it matters most, managers comply least. [academic | 2022] — [Human-Centered AI Lab summary](https://human-centered-ailab.github.io/lab-website-template/2022/05/17/Liang-Xu.html)
- After a stockout, managers push orders below the system's proposal, which causes further stockouts; blocking downward changes would have reduced these "self-inflicted" stockouts. [peer-reviewed, M&SOM | 2023] — [INFORMS](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/full/10.1287/msom.2023.0426)
- A 12-month retail experiment followed managers' overrides in some stores and ignored them in a random set of others, to measure what overrides are worth. [academic | secondary summary only] — [George Mason University](https://www.gmu.edu/news/2021-12/algorithmic-workplace-how-can-humans-excel)
- In vending operations, constrained overrides cut inventory by 1.28% without harming sales. [academic preprint | 2026; vending, not shops] — [arXiv 2607.00420](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00420)

Over-reliance and deskilling
- A review of 35 peer-reviewed studies finds automation bias, over-reliance on automated recommendations, consistently across healthcare, law and public administration. [academic review, AI and Society | 2025] — [University of Catania repository](https://www.iris.unict.it/retrieve/d096f7ef-6ba2-4684-a598-85fb55228c10/2025_AI%26amp%3bSociety.pdf)
- A year-long study of an AI deployment (42 participants, radiation oncology) describes hands-on skill declining as staff shifted from producing work to checking AI output. [academic preprint, under review | 2026; clinical] — [arXiv 2601.21920](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2601.21920)
- Workers are penalised for ignoring AI advice even when they know better, which pushes them towards over-reliance. [business-school report via trade press] — [TechRadar](https://techradar.com/pro/workers-are-being-punished-for-ignoring-ai-advice-even-when-they-know-better)
- Many workers fear dependence on AI more than losing their job. [vendor survey via trade press] — [HCA Magazine](https://www.hcamag.com/ca/specialization/transformation/many-workers-fear-ai-dependency-more-than-job-loss-report/586862)

### Inferences
- The replenishment research is the closest thing to hard evidence for this product, and its lesson is neither "trust the machine" nor "keep the human in charge". Managers know things the system does not (who is on, what the cellar can take, a local event), and they also have predictable biases (over-correcting after running out). The designs that work let the person adjust within limits, ask for a reason, record it, and feed it back. Applied here: proposed orders and rotas with editable lines and a visible "why", not a yes-or-no on the whole thing.
- A likely ordering of tasks by willingness to delegate, inferred from the above rather than measured: (a) reading and summarising, widely accepted; (b) drafting for review, such as a rota, an order or a report, accepted and already the main use; (c) acting unattended on reversible, low-cost items, such as reminders or re-sending an email, plausible; (d) acting unattended on money, pay, prices or anything safety-related, not supported by any evidence found. Staff objections to AI rotas show that even (b) needs explaining to the people it affects, not just the manager who approves it.
- Confirmation screens are not a cure for automation bias. The reviews show people approve what a system proposes, more so under time pressure, which describes a dinner service. A confirm step that shows only "Do this? Yes / No" will be tapped through. It needs to show the consequence in the terms the person would check (the total, the name, the date, what changes from what to what), and high-cost actions need a second, different kind of check.
- Deskilling is a real long-run risk for the client's users in two places: cash reconciliation and ordering, where a GM who has only ever approved suggestions cannot spot a wrong one. Showing the working (why this quantity, what the variance is made of) is the practical defence, and doubles as training for a high-turnover workforce.
- "A human I can reach" was the top trust factor in two markets. For a small vendor that means visible, fast support and an obvious undo, not only good model behaviour.

### Gaps
- No survey was found that asks operators task by task what they would let software do unattended (ordering, scheduling, pricing, payroll, allergen information). The query was not run (search budget).
- Allergen information and AI: UK regulator guidance and any incident evidence were not retrieved (query not run). Given the legal and safety weight, the report should not make claims here without it.
- Algorithmic scheduling overrides by managers (as distinct from ordering), and payroll and pricing automation acceptance: not researched.
- Automation bias and deskilling studies specific to frontline retail or hospitality work: none found; the evidence is clinical and office-based and transfers by analogy only. Two further studies (an observational endoscopy deskilling study and a survey of 319 knowledge workers linking confidence in AI to less critical thinking) surfaced in extracts without confirmed pages.

## 8. Onboarding and time to value: why small operators abandon software; setup wizards, data import, templates; conversational onboarding

### Takeaway
The evidence found is thin, mostly older and not restaurant-specific: small businesses abandon software mainly because they never get into the habit of using it, most feel they do not get its full benefit, and "I need more training" is the top stated reason. No evidence, for or against, was found on conversational or AI-led onboarding.

### Cited Findings

[US | vendor and trade magazine benchmark | n=112 | 2026; the extract did not show the exact definition] — [Fourth PDF](https://www.fourth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/State_of_Restaurant_Operations_2026.pdf)
- The UK checklist market leader is described as built for "zero-training" use with pre-built compliance checklists. [competitor's description] — [Xenia](https://www.xenia.team/articles/restaurant-checklist-app)
- Onboarding never ends in this sector: a large share of new starters leave within 90 days (section 3). — [Morning Advertiser](https://www.morningadvertiser.co.uk/Article/2026/05/29/only-1-in-4-staff-remain-in-work-after-90-days-training-costs-up-70/)

### Inferences
- There are two onboardings and they need different answers. The tenant's setup (venues, menus, staff, suppliers, opening balances) is a one-off, done by an owner or office person at a desk, where import from what they already have (a menu PDF, a supplier price list, last year's rota) and ready-made templates remove the blank page. The staff member's onboarding is continuous, happens on shift on a shared device, and has to be near zero: do the task in front of you, ask if stuck.
- The Fourth finding (use of existing tools separates winners from losers more than AI does) and the old churn finding (non-use causes churn) point the same way: the job of an assistant in the first weeks is to get the basics used daily, not to showcase clever features. Time to the first real cash-up, first rota published and first order sent is the measure that matters.
- Conversational setup is plausible for this audience (it replaces a manual nobody reads and suits owners who think in their own words), but it is unproven, and a long chat is a poor way to enter structured data in bulk. The defensible position is a conversation that gathers the gist and then shows an editable, pre-filled form or table to check.

### Gaps
- Evidence on conversational or AI-led onboarding (completion, time to value, error rates), and on AI import of menus and price lists in restaurant systems: the query was not run (search budget). Nothing was found in earlier searches.
- Restaurant- or retail-specific churn and abandonment data: none found; vendors do not publish it. Two widely repeated statistics ("85% abandonment", "67% quit because of too many steps") appeared with no traceable source and should not be used.

## 9. What franchise and multi-unit operators need above the store: consistency, benchmarking, permissions, auditing of what staff and AI changed

### Takeaway
Above-store leaders put operational consistency first and report a large gap between the real-time visibility they want and what they have; fragmented systems are the usual cause. Nothing was found on how operators want AI-made changes audited, which is a gap in the published evidence rather than a sign it does not matter.

### Cited Findings
- Operational consistency is the headline priority of franchise leaders for 2026, alongside AI ambitions; 17% are prioritising compliance and audit readiness. [UK-based vendor, franchise and multi-site organisations, countries not shown | vendor survey | 189 franchise leaders and 242 employees | early 2026] — [Claromentis](https://www.claromentis.com/franchise-digital-workplace-report-2026); [IT Brief UK](https://itbrief.co.uk/story/franchise-leaders-balance-ai-ambitions-with-consistency)
- About 80% of multi-unit restaurant operators say real-time visibility of daily task completion is important and 36% have it; 38% have complete real-time visibility of overtime and compliance. [US | vendor survey | 300+ operators with decision authority | spring 2025] — [Crunchtime](https://www.crunchtime.com/blog/webinar-recap-2025-restaurant-growth-insights-report); [Crunchtime 2026 insights](https://www.crunchtime.com/blog/26-key-insights-for-every-multi-unit-operator-in-2026)
- The biggest difference between top- and bottom-tier operators is "tool discipline" (the share of general managers using operational tools as directed), not AI adoption, brand size or technology spend; average operational maturity is 63 out of 100; 64% have not deployed AI for operations. [US | vendor and trade magazine benchmark | n=112 restaurant leaders | 2026] — [Fourth PDF](https://www.fourth.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/State_of_Restaurant_Operations_2026.pdf); [Fourth summary](https://www.fourth.com/ebook/state-of-restaurant-operations)
- 48% of restaurant leaders say their systems are not fully integrated and 66% run at least four separate technology systems. [multi-country | vendor-commissioned survey | n=1,254 restaurant leaders | reported second-hand] — [Restroworks](https://www.restroworks.com/blog/?p=26139)
- Only 3% of US restaurant operators are implementing AI companywide. [US | trade body | November 2026] — [MarketScale](https://www.marketscale.com/industries/hospitality/only-32-of-restaurant-operators-run-ai-companywide-nra-survey-finds)
- Vendors frame the franchise tension as standardising operations without slowing franchisees down. [vendor article] — [Fourth](https://www.fourth.com/article/standardizing-operations-without-slowing-down-franchisees)

### Inferences
- The above-store user's question is "which of my sites is off, and why", not "show me everything". Visibility of task completion and compliance by site, ranked, with the exception on top, answers the stated gap (80% want, 36% have). This is the group for whom comparison tables and benchmarks earn their place.
- "Tool discipline" being the main separator means head office will judge the product by whether GMs use it as intended. Usage by site and by person is therefore a feature for the area manager, not just a metric for the vendor.
- An assistant that can change data makes the audit question sharper for franchisors, even though no survey yet asks it: who asked, on which device, what the assistant proposed, who confirmed, what changed. With shared devices and shared logins (section 2), "who" is the weak link. Recording the assistant's part separately from the person's is a low-cost way to stay ahead of a question operators will ask once something goes wrong.
- Permissions need to follow the franchise shape: a franchisor wants some things locked (brand standards, allergen data, core menu) and some things local (rota, ordering, sold-out items). The assistant must respect the same lines as the screens, and say plainly when it cannot do something because of them.

### Gaps
- No evidence found on what franchisors or area managers want from audit trails of AI actions, or on governance of AI changes in multi-site operations.
- Benchmarking practice (which measures, how often, league tables or not) and the franchisor-to-franchisee permission split: not researched beyond the vendor framing above.
- UK-specific multi-unit and franchise data is missing; the measured figures are US. The UK franchise trade body's surveys were not searched.
