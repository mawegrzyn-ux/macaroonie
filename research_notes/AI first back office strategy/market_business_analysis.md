Method note for the report writer (read first). Every figure below was seen on 22 November 2026. ## 2. Customer economics in the UK, 2025 and 2026

### Takeaway
UK operators are being squeezed between rising labour and property taxes and weak pricing power: the 100 largest restaurant groups made about 1.5% profit on revenue in the year to mid-2026, and licensed premises are closing at a net 19 a week with very high gross churn. That makes labour-saving and control tools valuable in principle, but it also means little cash, little appetite for long contracts and scepticism about tools that add cost before they save it. Branded QSR franchising (chicken, US brands) is the growth pocket.

### Inferences
- Top-100 margin: GBP 204m on GBP 13.3bn is 1.5%, down from 2.8% (GBP 365m on GBP 12.9bn). At a 1.5% margin, a site turning over GBP 1m makes about GBP 15,000; a software bill of GBP 200 a month is 16% of that profit. Buyers will ask for payback in months, and a saving of one labour hour a day at a site (about GBP 16 to 17 fully loaded at the National Living Wage, my arithmetic: GBP 12.71 plus holiday accrual, 15% employer NI and pension) is worth about GBP 500 a month, more than most UK list prices in section 3.
- The break-even for a GBP 100 a month add-on is about six frontline hours a month, or four to five manager hours. That is the unit the product's value claims should be stated in.
- Churn is the opportunity: about 2.5% of the licensed estate closed and 2.2% opened in a single quarter (Q3 2026). New operators taking over vacated premises choose systems from scratch, with no switching cost, and want low upfront cost and short terms.
- Growth is concentrated in branded, franchised QSR while independents and food-led venues shrink. Multi-unit franchisees are getting larger by acquisition (Adil/KFC), which raises the importance of above-store control and of fitting inside a franchisor's mandated stack.
- What makes buyers cautious: thin cash, 24 to 36 month contracts with incumbents (section 7), fear of trading disruption at a till change, and the fact that the cost shock is in wages and taxes that software cannot remove, only offset.
- UKHospitality's 2,076-closure forecast and NIQ's 977 net licensed closures measure different things (a lobbying model across hotels, pubs and restaurants versus an audited licensed-premises count net of openings). Use NIQ for facts and UKHospitality for sentiment.

### Gaps
- No UK figure for technology spend per site, or IT as a percentage of sales, was found. The vendor ARPU figures above are North America-weighted and include payments.
- No British Franchise Association statistics (number of franchise units, share that are multi-unit) were retrieved.
- No ONS series was read directly; the jobs figure is the ONS number as quoted by a trade body.
- Business rates detail for April 2026 (multipliers, the end of the 40% relief, revaluation effects) was only seen through trade-body statements. Altus Group figures were not retrieved.
- Search extracts hint at a UK Budget in late October 2026 (a two-year rates freeze, a GBP 500,000 rateable-value surtax, BRC's April 2027 estimate) and at changes in who holds the Treasury brief. I could not verify the date or contents, or whether April 2027 wage rates have been announced. Check GOV.UK before the report quotes forward-looking tax or wage figures.
- No single-site or small-group margin benchmark for 2026 was found; the 1.5% is for the 100 largest groups.

## 4. AI packaging and pricing

### Takeaway
In 2026 the base assistant is being given away (Shopify Sidekick, Square Managerbot in beta, Xero JAX chat, Toast IQ), while money is taken in three other ways: a general price rise justified by AI (Intuit), premium-tier gating (Sage Copilot), and separately priced agents that do a job, priced on value or usage (Toast IQ Grow, Intuit's consumption model, Xero's planned usage-linked pricing). Several large vendors say openly that they have not settled on a pricing model.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- The pattern is "assist free, act paid". A chat assistant that answers questions and navigates is table stakes and is not being charged for by anyone seen here. What is charged for is an agent that owns an outcome (marketing campaigns that lift sales, bookkeeping done for you), priced on value or consumption.
- For a new entrant with no installed base, including the assistant in every plan matches the market; trying to charge for it separately would be out of line with Square, Shopify and Xero. Paid extras should be tied to a measurable result (hours saved, sales lift, invoices processed) or to metered heavy use.
- Vendors are hedging: "no additional charge currently" plus reserved rights to reprice (Xero), "not ready to choose" (Block). Contract terms should keep the same freedom, with a fair-use allowance for the included assistant.
- Falling unit cost is being tracked and reported (Shopify's 28% drop in cost per conversation in one quarter), which is what makes bundling affordable.

## 8. Unit economics of an LLM assistant

### Takeaway
At November 2026 model prices, a tool-using staff assistant costs on the order of USD 0.003 a request on a small model and USD 0.04 to 0.09 on mid and upper tier models, so the same typical site (about 600 requests a month) costs roughly USD 2, USD 27 or USD 54 a month depending on the model. Against UK price points of GBP 69 to 265 per site per month, model choice and prompt caching decide whether the assistant is a rounding error (under 2% of revenue) or a fifth or more of it. These are my estimates from published token prices and stated assumptions; no vendor publishes its cost per user.

### Cited Findings
- Token prices, Anthropic first-party API, USD per million tokens, input / output (cached 3 November 2026 in the API reference bundled with this session): Claude Haiku 5.5 0.10 / 0.50 (for prompts up to 100,000 tokens; 0.50 / 2.50 beyond); Claude Sonnet 5.5 2.00 / 10.00 with cache reads at 0.10; Claude Opus 5.5 4.00 / 20.00 with cache reads at 0.20; Claude Fable 5.2 8.00 / 40.00 with cache reads at 0.20; older Claude Haiku 4.5 1.00 / 5.00. Cache writes cost about 1.25x the input price (5-minute lifetime) or 2x (1-hour); batch processing is 50% cheaper — [Anthropic pricing page](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/pricing.md); [Anthropic prompt caching documentation](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/build-with-claude/prompt-caching.md)
- A listed vendor treats unit cost as a managed metric: Shopify's cost per Sidekick conversation fell 28% between Q2 and Q3 2026 — [AlphaSense Q3 2026 summary](https://www.alpha-sense.com/earnings/shop/)
- Scale of use at a large vendor: nearly 34 million Sidekick conversations in Q2 2026 — [Retail TouchPoints](https://www.retailtouchpoints.com/news/shopify-credits-ai-for-34-revenue-growth-in-q2-2026/620805/)
- A software gross margin of 83% is reported by Lightspeed while AI handles over 80% of its support tickets — [Lightspeed Q4 FY26 call highlights](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/lightspeed-commerce-q4-earnings-call-151036920.html)
- UK revenue per site to compare against: Square Plus GBP 69, Toast GBP 80 to 150, Lightspeed GBP 79 to 219, Zonal GBP 199 to 265 per month (sources in section 3) — [Square UK restaurants pricing](https://squareup.com/gb/en/point-of-sale/restaurants/pricing); [Zonal 3-in-1 Tech Suite](https://www.zonal.co.uk/products/rental-packages/3-in-1-tech-suite-for-independents)

### Inferences
Worked estimate (all assumptions mine, stated so the writer can change them):
- One "request" = a staff member asks for something and the assistant finishes it with a short tool loop: 3 model calls, each re-reading a cached prefix of 20,000 tokens (system prompt plus a large tool catalogue), plus 3,000 new input tokens (conversation and tool results) and 400 output tokens per call. One cache write per five-request session.
- Cost per request: Haiku 5.5 about USD 0.003 (its cache-read price was not in the reference, so a tenth of the input price is assumed); Sonnet 5.5 about USD 0.045; Opus 5.5 about USD 0.09; Fable 5.2 about USD 0.15 or more (it always reasons, so output runs higher). Without prompt caching the Sonnet figure rises to about USD 0.15, more than three times as much (60,000 prefix tokens at USD 2.00 instead of USD 0.10 per million).
- Usage per site per month: light 100 requests (an occasional helper); typical 600 (four active staff, about five requests each per day); AI-first 3,000 (most navigation and changes go through the assistant, about 100 a day).

| Model | Light (100) | Typical (600) | AI-first (3,000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Haiku 5.5 | USD 0.30 | USD 1.80 | USD 9 |
| Sonnet 5.5 | USD 4.50 | USD 27 | USD 135 |
| Opus 5.5 | USD 9 | USD 54 | USD 270 |

- Share of subscription revenue, taking GBP 150 to 300 per site per month for a bundled platform and an illustrative GBP 1 = USD 1.30 (the rate is an assumption, not sourced): typical use is under 1% on Haiku 5.5, about 7% to 14% on Sonnet 5.5 (about GBP 21) and about 14% to 28% on Opus 5.5 (about GBP 42). AI-first use on Sonnet 5.5 (about GBP 104) would take 35% to 70% of revenue; on Haiku 5.5 (about GBP 7) 2% to 5%.
- Per active user: at five requests a working day (about 110 a month), roughly USD 0.35 on Haiku 5.5, USD 5 on Sonnet 5.5, USD 10 on Opus 5.5 per user per month.
- Consequence: an AI-first product at UK price points only works with routing (a small model for navigation, look-ups and form filling; a larger one for analysis and multi-step changes), aggressive prompt caching, a trimmed tool list per request, and a fair-use cap. A software gross margin target near the 80%+ that listed peers report leaves at most a few pounds per site per month for inference at a GBP 150 price.
- Costs not in the table: speech-to-text for voice, document reading (menus, invoices, receipts, priced per page or image), retries, evaluation runs, and background jobs (briefings, scheduled reports). Document reading scales with invoices, not with users, and may be the larger line for purchasing-heavy sites.
- Token prices have been falling (the current small model is a tenth of the price of its predecessor in the table above), so a cost that is marginal today is likely to be comfortable within a year; pricing decisions should not lock in today's cost as a customer-facing surcharge.

### Gaps
- Prices for other providers' models (OpenAI, Google) were not retrieved; the search budget ran out. The Anthropic figures come from the reference bundled with this session (dated 3 November 2026), not a live read of the pricing page.
- No vendor in this sector publishes cost per active user, per conversation or per location; Shopify gives only the percentage change.
- No published AI gross-margin benchmark (for example from ICONIQ or Bessemer) was retrieved.

- An exchange rate was assumed, not sourced.

### Inferences
A scorecard for an AI-first back office, built from what vendors report plus what they leave out (targets are my suggestions, anchored where a benchmark exists):
- Activation: share of sites where a staff member completes a first real task through the assistant within 14 days of go-live. No external benchmark.
- Habit: weekly active sites and weekly active staff using the assistant, as a share of all active sites and staff. Anchor: about a quarter of Toast locations weekly; aim for 25% to 40% as an AI-first product should beat a bolt-on.
- Depth: share of all changes (records created or edited) made through the assistant versus the screens, by area. Anchor: two-thirds for one Shopify workflow. This is the truest "AI-first" measure, and it should be tracked per area because it will vary widely.
- Task success: share of assistant sessions that end in the intended result without the user abandoning to a screen or repeating the request; measured from logs and a sampled human review. No published benchmark.
- Reversal and error rate: share of assistant-made changes undone, corrected or overwritten within 24 hours, compared with the same rate for manual changes. No published benchmark; this is the trust measure Shopify's critics point at.
- Confirmation behaviour: share of proposed write actions approved, edited or rejected at the confirm step.
- Time saved: median time to complete the ten most common tasks through the assistant versus through the screens, measured in the product, and converted to hours per site per month (the unit in section 2).
- Containment: share of "how do I" questions answered in product with no support ticket; tickets per site per month. Anchor: over 80% at Lightspeed.
- Unit cost: model cost per request, per active user and per site per month, and as a share of revenue (section 8). Anchor: Shopify reports the trend only.
- Retention and expansion: logo and revenue retention for sites that use the assistant weekly versus those that do not, and attach of any paid agent. Anchor: about 20% attach at Lightspeed.
- Outcome proof: one measured operator result per paid capability (Toast's 8% sales lift is the model), gathered from pilots before pricing it.
Pre-production, the usable subset is task success, reversal rate, time per task and unit cost on test tenants; the adoption measures need live customers.

### Gaps

From background knowledge, unverified here: customer-service agent vendors publish average resolution rates (Intercom has cited figures in the 50% to 65% range for Fin), and coding assistants publish suggestion acceptance rates; neither transfers directly to back-office tasks.
- No vendor in restaurant or retail technology publishes task success, error, reversal or time-saved figures for its assistant.
- No evidence was found on how franchise groups themselves measure a vendor's AI (for example in tenders).

## 10. Strategic options and what looks defensible for a small new entrant

### Takeaway
Four positions are visible: the assistant as a free feature of a large platform (Shopify, Square, Xero, Toast IQ), paid agents sold on value on top of that platform (Toast IQ Grow, Intuit), AI-native operations layers on top of someone else's till (Nory, Loop AI, MarginEdge), and platform plus ecosystem (Shopify's apps and automations). For a small UK entrant the first is undifferentiated and the third is a funding race; the more defensible ground on this evidence is a narrow segment the big platforms serve poorly (franchised and multi-unit QSR needing above-store control), with the assistant included, flexible terms, and proof of labour hours saved.

### Inferences
Assessment of each option for a small, pre-revenue UK entrant:
- Assistant as a feature: necessary, not sufficient. It is being given away by platforms with a million sellers of usage data. It cannot be the reason to buy, and it cannot carry a separate price.
- AI-native rebuild of the whole stack ("AI-first"): the entrant's one structural advantage is that it owns every module and its data in one schema, so an assistant can read and act across till, rota, purchasing and food safety, where incumbents stitch acquisitions together and operations-layer startups depend on third-party POS integrations. That advantage is real only if cross-module tasks (for example "tomorrow's delivery is short, change the menu, the order and the rota") work reliably; it is an engineering claim to be proved, and the market evidence does not yet show buyers paying for "AI-first" as such.
- Vertical agent sold on outcomes: this is where incumbents are finding revenue (Toast IQ Grow), but outcome pricing needs measured proof and enough customers to measure on. It is a second-stage move for a pre-production product: build the measurement first (section 9), then price one agent on its result.
- Platform plus ecosystem: not open to a newcomer; ecosystems follow installed base.
- Segment focus: the most defensible near-term position is a specific buyer the large platforms handle poorly. Evidence points to franchised and multi-unit QSR: it is the growing part of UK eating out, it needs brand-level control over franchisee operations, it is excluded from Square's agent roll-out for now, and new franchise systems choose a stack once for all future units. The enterprise suites that do serve it (Zonal, Access, Vita Mojo) are quote-priced with long terms.
- What a small entrant can defend: (1) depth of fit to one segment's workflows and above-store model; (2) one data model across modules, which makes the assistant more capable per pound of engineering; (3) commercial terms incumbents will not match (no lock-in, transparent per-site price, the assistant included); (4) a measured hours-saved claim in a market where a saved labour hour is worth about GBP 16 to 17 and margins are 1.5%. What it cannot defend: model quality (everyone rents the same models), a generic chat assistant, or payments-subsidised pricing.
- Retail expansion: the SMB retail field is led by Shopify POS, Square and Lightspeed, each with a bundled assistant, ecommerce and payments. Nothing in this evidence shows an opening for a hospitality-first newcomer in general retail. If pursued at all, the adjacent case is food-led retail and franchise retail that shares the hospitality workflows (rota, food safety, purchasing, multi-unit control); treat this as a hypothesis to test, not a finding.
- Main risks to flag in the report: concentration risk for the buyer (a small vendor running the till), the funded AI-native competitors moving down-market or into the UK mid-market (Nory is already UK-based), incumbents adding assistants to existing suites within a contract cycle, and inference cost if "AI-first" is taken literally at UK price points (section 8).
